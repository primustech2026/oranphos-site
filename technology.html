Thermal acid · process control

Purity is decided at the acid, not at the filter

The useful comparison is not “white crystal versus white crystal.” It is which impurities entered the acid before the first neutralization valve opened.

Mechanism

Two acid routes create two different impurity starting points.

A 25 t MKP container may look uniform at the port, but its upstream acid can come from 2 very different chemical routes. Wet-process phosphoric acid starts with phosphate rock and sulfuric acid, while thermal acid starts with refined yellow phosphorus burned to phosphorus pentoxide and hydrated under controlled conditions. Both routes produce H₃PO₄, yet they do not carry the same burden of calcium, magnesium, iron, aluminum, fluorine, sulfate and trace metals into the neutralization stage. That upstream burden determines how much purification the producer must perform before potassium hydroxide or potassium carbonate is added.

In the wet route, finely ground phosphate rock reacts with H₂SO₄ and forms phosphoric acid plus calcium sulfate. Rock is a geological material, so 1 ore body can contain a shifting mix of Fe, Al, Mg, Ca, F, silica and heavy-metal traces. Filtration removes the main gypsum phase, but “filtered” does not mean that every dissolved ion or fine suspended particle is gone. A fertilizer-grade acid plant must choose which impurities to remove, how deeply to remove them and whether the economics of that extra purification fit the selling price of the finished MKP.

Wet-process acid can be purified to high standards, and buyers should not treat “wet” as an automatic failure. The engineering point is that purification is an added train: clarification, solvent extraction, precipitation, ion exchange or combinations of these steps may be required before 85% H₃PO₄ is suitable for a low-residue salt. Each stage consumes chemicals, creates a side stream and adds another control limit. If a commodity MKP line starts from acid intended mainly for bulk fertilizer, the producer may accept higher insolubles or metals because the target application is soil broadcasting rather than a 0.8 mm drip emitter.

The thermal route changes the starting material before acid exists. Yellow phosphorus is produced separately, then burned with controlled air to P₂O₅ and hydrated to H₃PO₄. Nonvolatile mineral components present in phosphate rock do not follow phosphorus through the same refining and combustion path at comparable levels. The resulting acid therefore begins with a lower load of iron, aluminum, calcium, magnesium and suspended mineral matter. ORANPHOS operates a 20,000 t/y thermal acid line under DCS and SIS control, so gas flow, combustion, hydration and heat recovery are managed as one system rather than purchased as an outside feedstock.

That cleaner starting point matters during neutralization. Potassium hydroxide or potassium carbonate is dosed against H₃PO₄ to reach the KH₂PO₄ stoichiometric window, and the reaction liquor is then clarified and concentrated. If the acid carries calcium or magnesium, increasing pH can push those ions toward phosphate precipitates exactly when the producer is trying to form a clean soluble salt. Fine calcium phosphate, iron phosphate or silica-bearing material can pass through an imperfect separation step and later appear as “water insolubles” on the COA. A final filter is therefore a control barrier, not a machine that can erase the chemistry of the incoming acid.

Crystallization adds another separation opportunity, but it is not a universal correction. Temperature, supersaturation, residence time and agitation determine crystal habit, yield and how much mother liquor remains on the surface. A producer can wash crystals and remove part of the soluble impurity load, yet every extra wash loses product and creates more recycle liquor. Chloride is especially relevant because it remains soluble; it does not simply disappear with a filter cake. ORANPHOS lists typical MKP chloride at 0.0014%, so a buyer can compare a measured lot value with the purchasing ceiling instead of accepting the phrase “low chloride.”

The same logic applies to heavy metals. Thermal processing is not a substitute for analysis, because contamination can still enter through yellow phosphorus, alkali, process water, equipment or handling. It does, however, reduce the mineral impurity burden the downstream salt plant must manage. Each lot should still be checked against its intended market: fertilizer, food, feed or electronic use does not share one universal metals panel. A serious qualification therefore asks for 10 consecutive COAs, the test method, detection limits and an SGS sample from the loaded trial container—not a single perfect certificate chosen from the archive.

For a fertigation buyer, the practical result appears in 3 places: stock-tank clarity, filter differential pressure and emitter uniformity. MKP at 0.07% water insolubles leaves less non-dissolving material than a 0.10% ceiling, but tank design and mixing discipline still matter. Hard water, calcium nitrate in the same concentrate, high pH, cold-night recrystallization or contaminated transfer equipment can create deposits even when the original bag passes its COA. The acid route controls the product’s starting risk; the farm or fertilizer plant must preserve that advantage through correct A/B separation and dilution.

Cost comparisons should therefore use application risk, not only price per tonne. A commodity lot that is USD 20/t cheaper can become expensive if 1 blocked irrigation zone creates uneven fruit set, labor for acid cleaning or a customer complaint. Conversely, a thermal-process product should not earn a premium solely from its process label. Ask for assay, insolubles, chloride, moisture, pH and relevant metals across actual lots, then verify the first 25 t container. Process explains why a result is plausible; batch data proves whether the supplier is delivering it.

Five controlled steps

Yellow phosphorus to finished phosphate.

The route is drawn as 5 blocks, but each block has its own inputs, release limits and retained records.

Yellow phosphorus

Qualified elemental P feed enters the 20,000 t/y acid line.

Thermal acid

Combustion, hydration, DCS/SIS control and heat recovery produce H₃PO₄.

Neutralization

KOH or K₂CO₃ is dosed to the KH₂PO₄ reaction window.

Crystallization

Concentration, cooling, separation and washing set crystal quality.

Finished product

Drying, COA release, 25 kg PE-lined bags and 1,000-bag loading.

The gram-level calculation

A 0.03-point difference becomes 90 g in one batch.

A greenhouse manager dissolving 300 kg of MKP at 0.07% water insolubles introduces a theoretical maximum of 300 kg × 0.0007 = 0.210 kg, or 210 g, of material that does not dissolve under the test condition. At a 0.10% ceiling, the same 300 kg introduces 300 kg × 0.0010 = 0.300 kg, or 300 g.

The difference is 90 g per 300 kg batch. Expressed against the 300 g commodity reference, 210 g is a 30% reduction in theoretical insoluble load. Across 20 batches, that is 1.8 kg less material presented to the tank, screen filter and emitter network.

What the calculation does not prove

Insolubles are a load estimate, not a dripper warranty.

The 210 g and 300 g figures translate a COA percentage into mass, but they do not predict particle-size distribution or where each particle will settle. A 120-mesh screen, a disc filter and a 0.8 mm labyrinth do not retain material in the same way. Some residue stays in the B tank, some reaches the filter and some may combine with calcium, iron or carbonate already present in the water.

A useful validation therefore runs the exact crop-water recipe at the intended stock concentration. Dissolve the MKP below the 15–20 kg/100 L cold-night guideline, keep calcium nitrate in the A tank and record clarity after 2 hours and after overnight cooling. Then inspect filter differential pressure and flush-end residue during a controlled 1-zone trial. The product COA narrows the risk; the farm test confirms the complete system.
