MKP vs DKP vs urea phosphate: choosing your P-K source

Three white materials, 3 different nutrient jobs and 1 container strategy that often makes sense.

8 min read

A formulator places 3 spec sheets side by side: MKP 0-52-34, DKP K₂HPO₄ and urea phosphate 17-44-0. All 3 supply phosphorus, but only 2 supply potassium and only 1 supplies nitrogen. The correct purchase depends on nutrient balance, stock-tank pH, solubility and the other salts already present in the recipe.

What does an MKP vs DKP fertilizer comparison show?

MKP is the neutral-to-mildly acidic P-K base, DKP supplies more potassium with an alkaline reaction, and urea phosphate combines 17% nitrogen with 44% P₂O₅ and strong acidity. At 20°C, reference solubilities are about 22.6, 168 and 370 g/100 g water respectively, so tank design matters.

Where does MKP earn its place?

MKP gives a clean P-K input without nitrogen, which is valuable when the crop already receives enough nitrate from calcium nitrate or potassium nitrate. Its 0-52-34 declaration makes formulation math straightforward, while a pH near 4.5 is usually easier to place in a B tank than an alkaline phosphate. The main constraint is solubility: 22.6 g/100 g water at 20°C requires a cold-night safety margin in concentrated stock.

For greenhouse and hydroponic work, insolubles and chloride often separate one MKP grade from another more clearly than the 99% assay. A 300 kg batch at 0.07% water insolubles carries a theoretical 210 g residue load, compared with 300 g at 0.10%. Review those lot values in the MKP 0-52-34 technical specification before comparing only the FOB price per tonne.

When is DKP the better K source?

DKP carries 2 potassium atoms per phosphate unit and reacts alkaline, so it fits formulas that need more K and buffering above neutral pH. Its 168 g/100 g water reference solubility at 20°C permits much denser concentrates than MKP. That advantage can reduce tank volume, but it also makes pH and compatibility control more important around magnesium, calcium and non-chelated micronutrients.

DKP is not a drop-in replacement for MKP on a kilogram basis. The nutrient ratios differ, the pH direction reverses and the delivered K₂O contribution must be recalculated. If a formulator replaces 100 kg MKP with 100 kg DKP without rebuilding the full analysis, both the nutrient guarantee and the stock-tank chemistry can move outside the original window.

What problem does urea phosphate solve?

Urea phosphate combines 17% nitrogen and 44% P₂O₅ with a strong acidifying reaction. In high-bicarbonate water, that acidity can help lower solution pH and reduce carbonate scale, while its reported 370 g/100 g water solubility at 20°C supports compact stock solutions. It is useful where the formula needs N and P together but does not need additional K from the same material.

UP is still a phosphate, so calcium nitrate remains a prohibited partner in concentrated stock. Its acidity also changes how micronutrient chelates, pumps, seals and tank materials should be evaluated. A 1 L jar test should use the actual source water and final planned ratios, followed by a 12-hour check for heat, haze, sediment or crystal formation.

How should a formulator choose among the 3?

  1. Set the required kilograms of N, P₂O₅ and K₂O per 1,000 kg finished fertilizer or 1,000 L working solution.
  2. Decide whether the stock needs an acidic, mildly acidic or alkaline reaction before selecting UP, MKP or DKP.
  3. Check 20°C and minimum-night solubility against the actual B-tank concentration.
  4. Rebuild calcium, magnesium, sulfate and micronutrient compatibility around the selected phosphate.
  5. Compare delivered nutrient cost, 25 t container loading and lot-level COA stability—not only USD/t.

A mixed-container strategy often reduces commercial risk. Loading MKP and urea phosphate in 1 FCL gives a distributor 2 core fertigation inputs: a P-K base and an acidifying N-P material. With 25 kg PE-lined bags, the allocation can be set in 1,000 kg increments while preserving one supplier qualification, one document set and one 10–14 day production window.

MKP plus DKP can also make sense for formulators serving both acidic and alkaline product families, but the smaller DKP market may affect minimum quantities. Ask for separate COAs and bag markings even when both products share 1 container. One B/L does not mean the 2 salts share a specification or can be blended without a formula check.

Key Takeaways

  • Use MKP for a nitrogen-free P-K base, DKP for higher K with alkaline buffering and UP for acidifying N-P.
  • At 20°C, 22.6, 168 and 370 g/100 g water imply very different stock-tank limits.
  • Recalculate the full nutrient analysis before replacing 100 kg of one phosphate with another.
  • MKP + UP in 1 FCL can cover 2 core inputs with one supplier and document set.
